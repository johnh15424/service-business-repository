DOG GROOMING BUSINESS COSTS

Know what every groom
actually costs.

A busy diary can still hide weak pricing. The useful number is not just what you charge, but what is left after the time, products and business costs behind the appointment.

Calculate your own groom price ↗

Start with labour, including your own

Labour is usually the biggest appointment-level cost. Use the real amount of time required for bathing, drying, clipping, scissoring, finishing, handling, cleaning and, for mobile groomers, travel. If two people work on the dog at the same time, count person-minutes rather than clock time.

If you work for yourself, your time still has a cost. Treating owner labour as free can make a price look profitable when it is only replacing your unpaid time.

Products are small individually, but recurring

Shampoo, conditioner, ear cleaner, wipes, finishing sprays, bows, bandanas and specialist coat products should be converted into a cost per groom. A simple method is pack cost divided by the number of usable appointments, adjusted for actual usage.

For concentrated products, use the cost of concentrate consumed, not the volume of diluted solution.

Equipment wear belongs in the price too

Blades, sharpening, clipper maintenance, dryers, tables and replacement tools do not disappear simply because they are not bought every week. Allocate a modest equipment-wear amount per appointment so future maintenance and replacement are funded by the work that causes the wear.

Allocate fixed overhead across realistic paid appointments

Rent, insurance, booking software, accounting, phone, subscriptions and other fixed costs exist whether a day is full or quiet. One practical planning method is to divide monthly fixed overhead by the number of completed, paid appointments you realistically expect in a month.

Overhead per appointment = monthly fixed overhead ÷ expected paid appointments

Use realistic completed jobs rather than maximum booking capacity. Otherwise the overhead allowance can be artificially low.

Mobile groomers need route economics

Mobile grooming adds costs a fixed salon does not carry in the same way: vehicle depreciation, fuel, commercial insurance, maintenance and travel time. Route density matters too. Two appointments with the same grooming time can have very different economics if one adds substantial driving.

The calculator therefore keeps travel distance and travel time separate from grooming time so you can see what mobility adds to the appointment.

Do not forget payment and booking fees

Card processors and booking platforms can charge both fixed and percentage fees. A percentage fee linked to the final customer payment should be included when solving for the selling price rather than subtracted as an afterthought.

Tax is not profit

If you are required to charge VAT, GST, HST or sales tax, keep that tax separate from your business profit. The calculator solves a pre-tax price first, then applies the selected tax treatment. Registration and taxability vary by jurisdiction, so verify your own position.

Turn the cost base into a selling price

Once the cost base is complete, apply your target margin and any realistic contingency for overruns or rework. Margin and markup are different. A 40% margin requires a higher selling price than adding a 40% markup to cost.

See the full pricing formula and margin explanation.

Use your own numbers

There is no single correct grooming price across countries, cities or business models. A cost-based floor gives you a better starting point than copying another salon whose wages, rent, capacity and service mix may be completely different.
