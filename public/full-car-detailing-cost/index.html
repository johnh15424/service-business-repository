FOR DETAILING BUSINESSES

How to price a full car detail from the actual package workload

This guide is for detailers building profitable full-detail packages, not vehicle owners comparing local prices. A full detail must be defined by scope first, then priced from labour stages, condition, products, equipment and business overhead.

“Full detail” needs a precise scope

One business may define a full detail as a thorough interior and exterior clean. Another may include decontamination, machine polishing, stain extraction or protection. If the package name is vague, the customer and detailer can have different expectations while the price remains fixed.

Document the included stages: pre-wash, contact wash, wheels, decontamination, drying, interior vacuum, plastics, glass, extraction, leather treatment, paint enhancement and protection where applicable. Price only after the work is defined.

Break the package into labour stages

A large package is easier to price when time is estimated by stage. Exterior preparation, wheels, wash and decontamination may have one productivity pattern. Interior reset, extraction and stain treatment have another. Paint enhancement or correction can dominate the job entirely.

Estimate total paid person-hours. If two detailers work together, count both. Add setup, pack-down and travel for mobile businesses.

Vehicle condition changes the economics

A maintained vehicle and a neglected vehicle should not automatically share the same labour assumption. Pet hair, embedded sand, spills, heavy brake dust, tar or water spotting can increase time materially. Large SUVs and vans also increase surface area, interior volume and product consumption.

Use a standard package for a defined condition band, then quote additional labour when the vehicle sits outside it. This is clearer than absorbing every difficult car into the base price.

Products and equipment need recovery

Full details can use more chemicals, towels, pads and machine time than maintenance work. Decontamination products, compounds, polishes and protection products may be significant direct costs. Pad cleaning, towel laundry, extractor wear, polisher maintenance and vacuum replacement also belong somewhere in the pricing model.

Correction work should be treated separately

Paint enhancement and multi-stage correction are not merely longer washes. They require inspection, machine work, pads, compounds and skill, with results limited by paint condition and safe correction depth. If correction is included in a package, define the expected outcome and estimate the labour separately.

Full-detail pricing sequence

  1. Define the package and exclusions.
  2. Assess vehicle size and condition.
  3. Estimate labour by interior, exterior and correction stages.
  4. Add chemicals, towels, pads, water, power and equipment wear.
  5. Add travel and vehicle cost for mobile work.
  6. Allocate a realistic share of overhead.
  7. Add transaction fees and contingency where relevant.
  8. Apply the retained margin target and minimum charge.
  9. Add tax only where required.

Package pricing should protect capacity

A six-hour detail occupies a large part of the working day. The price must therefore cover not only direct cost but the opportunity cost of the capacity used. A package that appears profitable per job can still weaken the business if it prevents more valuable work from fitting into the schedule.

Review revenue and retained profit per productive hour alongside the headline package price.

Common full-detail pricing mistakes

  • using “full detail” without defining the work
  • including correction without pricing the extra hours
  • ignoring vehicle condition until the job starts
  • forgetting towel, pad and machine wear
  • treating mobile travel as free
  • pricing large packages from competitor menus rather than internal costs
