FOR WINDOW CLEANING BUSINESSES

How to price window cleaning from access, method and labour time

This guide is for window-cleaning businesses setting profitable prices, not homeowners comparing quotes. Window count is useful, but access, pane type, cleaning method, travel and route density often have a larger effect on the real job cost.

Window count is only the first layer

Ten large accessible panes can be faster than ten divided-light windows with frames, sills and awkward interior access. Conservatories, roof glass, extensions, locked gates and parking constraints also change the visit.

Use window count to structure the quote, but estimate paid time from the actual property and method.

Access determines productivity

Ground-floor glass, standard first-floor windows, pole work and specialist access should not automatically share the same unit price. Height and positioning can reduce cleaning speed, increase setup time and require different equipment. Where ladders or specialist access are used, follow applicable safety requirements and build the additional time into the quote.

Traditional and water-fed-pole work have different costs

Traditional cleaning can involve more direct access and detailing time. Water-fed-pole systems can improve exterior productivity but bring pure-water production, resin, filters, pumps, poles, hose and vehicle-system wear. The business should recover the method it actually uses rather than assuming the equipment is free once purchased.

Interior work changes the visit

Inside cleaning often involves customer access, furniture, blinds, floor protection and more careful movement through the property. If interior and exterior cleaning are both included, estimate them separately before combining the price.

Route density is a pricing advantage

Recurring residential window cleaning can become highly efficient when customers are close together. Record drive time, parking time and service time by route. A nearby repeat customer may be profitable at a different minimum than a one-off property at the edge of the service area.

A practical window-cleaning pricing sequence

  1. Define inside, outside, frames, sills and any extras.
  2. Assess pane count, size and access.
  3. Estimate total paid crew-hours.
  4. Add pure water, consumables and equipment wear.
  5. Add travel and vehicle cost.
  6. Allocate business overhead.
  7. Apply fees, contingency, minimum charge and retained margin.
  8. Add tax only where required.

Per-window pricing can still be useful

After the full job price is calculated, divide by the number of windows or panes if a unit rate helps with future estimating. Maintain separate internal categories when access or pane complexity changes materially. The unit rate should reflect the work, not replace the costing model.

Common window-cleaning pricing mistakes

  • charging the same per-window price regardless of access
  • forgetting frames, sills or interior work in the scope
  • not recovering pure-water and equipment costs
  • ignoring parking and travel time
  • using the same minimum for dense routes and isolated one-off work
  • copying competitor prices without comparing service scope
