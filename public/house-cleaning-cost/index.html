FOR CLEANING BUSINESSES

How to calculate house cleaning cost and set a profitable price

This guide is for cleaning businesses setting prices, not homeowners comparing local cleaners. A profitable house-cleaning quote starts with paid cleaner-hours, job-level supplies, travel and overhead, then protects a minimum charge and retained margin.

Cleaner-hours matter more than room count

Bedrooms and bathrooms are useful for scoping, but labour is the main economic driver. A three-bedroom home that is regularly maintained can take less time than a smaller property with heavy kitchen grease, pet hair, clutter or a first-time reset. Pricing only from room count can therefore produce inconsistent margins.

Estimate the total person-hours required. Two cleaners working for two hours is four cleaner-hours. Include paid travel if staff are on the clock between jobs, and include setup, key collection or linen handling when those tasks create real paid time.

Build the quote from the real cost base

A reliable house-cleaning price should recover more than wages. The job also uses chemicals, cloths, bags, gloves, laundry, vacuum and mop wear, fuel or vehicle cost, payment fees and a share of fixed business overhead.

Monthly overhead can include insurance, software, phones, storage, admin, marketing and management time. Allocate that overhead across realistic paid jobs rather than theoretical maximum capacity. If the diary usually completes 80 paid visits per month, dividing overhead by 150 possible slots will under-recover the business cost.

Fixed price or hourly price?

Either customer-facing model can work. The key is to maintain an internal cost model underneath it.

Hourly pricing can protect the business when scope is uncertain, but customers may dislike open-ended totals. Fixed job pricing is easier for the customer to understand, but the cleaner must estimate time accurately. Many established businesses use fixed customer prices while still calculating the expected cleaner-hours internally.

Recurring cleans should not be priced like first-time cleans

Recurring work often becomes more predictable because the property is maintained to a known standard and the cleaner learns the layout. First-time cleans can involve extra kitchen build-up, bathroom scale, dust accumulation and slower navigation around unfamiliar rooms.

Use separate service assumptions for recurring maintenance, standard cleans, deep cleans, move-in or move-out work and short-stay turnovers. That keeps one difficult service from distorting the price of another.

Minimum charges protect short jobs

A small apartment can still require booking administration, travel, loading supplies and payment processing. If the calculated price falls below the business's viable call-out level, a minimum charge prevents short visits from consuming capacity at an uneconomic price.

The minimum should come from the business's own fixed and variable costs. It is not a universal market number.

A practical pricing sequence

  1. Estimate total paid cleaner-hours.
  2. Multiply by the loaded labour cost.
  3. Add supplies, laundry and equipment wear.
  4. Add travel and vehicle cost where relevant.
  5. Allocate a realistic share of monthly overhead.
  6. Add transaction fees and contingency where appropriate.
  7. Apply the retained margin target.
  8. Compare the result with the minimum charge.
  9. Add tax only where the business must charge it.

Common house-cleaning pricing mistakes

  • using the owner's time as free labour
  • pricing by bedrooms without checking expected hours
  • forgetting paid travel between customers
  • treating supplies and laundry as negligible
  • dividing overhead by unrealistic capacity
  • offering a deep clean at a recurring-clean price
  • confusing markup with retained margin

Use actual job records to improve future pricing

The first estimate is a model. The best cleaning businesses refine that model using completed-job data. Compare estimated cleaner-hours with actual hours, note repeat problem areas and review supply usage. If a service type repeatedly overruns, adjust the pricing assumptions instead of relying on staff to work faster indefinitely.
