FOR HANDYMAN BUSINESSES

How to turn a handyman scope into a profitable fixed job price

This guide is for handyman businesses building fixed quotes, not customers comparing local tradespeople. A fixed price should come from estimated labour, materials, collection time, travel, tools, overhead and scope risk rather than a rough guess.

Fixed price does not mean fixed cost

The customer sees one number, but the business still needs an internal cost breakdown. A simple repair may require investigation, travel, material collection and cleanup. A larger installation may involve several work stages and multiple supplier runs.

Define the task precisely enough that the labour estimate has meaning. State what is included, what materials are assumed and what conditions would trigger a revised quote.

Estimate labour in stages

Break the job into setup, removal, preparation, installation, finishing and cleanup. Add material collection or delivery time where the business is responsible for it. This is more reliable than guessing one total number of hours, especially on multi-step work.

Use loaded labour cost rather than wage alone. For owner-operators, include a realistic cost for the owner's working time before calculating business profit.

Handle materials deliberately

Materials can be passed through at cost, marked up or combined with a procurement or collection charge. The commercial method can vary, but the business should recover the time and cost created by sourcing them.

Small fixings and consumables are often forgotten: screws, plugs, sealant, blades, abrasives and protective materials can add up across many jobs. Include a sensible allowance or charge them directly.

Scope uncertainty needs protection

Some handyman jobs reveal hidden damage, unsuitable substrates or previous poor workmanship once work begins. Where uncertainty is material, define assumptions and exclusions before the customer accepts the quote. Avoid pricing unlimited investigation into a fixed fee unless the contingency can support it.

For genuinely uncertain tasks, an hourly or diagnostic-first approach may be more suitable.

Travel, parking and specialist tools belong in the job

City parking, tolls, long travel and specialist tool hire can change the economics. Add job-specific costs directly. General vehicle and tool replacement can sit in overhead if that is how the business accounts for them, but make sure nothing disappears between categories.

A practical fixed-job pricing sequence

  1. Write the scope and exclusions.
  2. Estimate paid labour by work stage.
  3. Add material cost and sourcing time.
  4. Add consumables, tool hire, travel and parking.
  5. Allocate business overhead.
  6. Add payment fees and contingency where appropriate.
  7. Apply the target retained margin and minimum charge.
  8. Add tax only where required.

Check the effective hourly return

After setting the fixed price, divide the pre-tax price by expected paid hours. This gives an effective hourly selling rate that can be compared with the business's internal hourly benchmark. If the fixed job produces a much lower hourly return, revisit the estimate before sending the quote.

Common fixed-price mistakes

  • quoting before the scope is clear
  • forgetting material collection time
  • absorbing parking and travel
  • including specialist tools without recovery
  • pricing uncertainty as if every job is straightforward
  • not checking the finished quote against the hourly benchmark
