FOR MOBILE DETAILING BUSINESSES

How to price mobile car detailing from the full visit cost

This guide is for detailers setting profitable prices, not vehicle owners comparing local quotes. Mobile detailing should recover hands-on labour, setup, travel, chemicals, water, power, equipment wear and overhead before margin is added.

Mobile detailing has costs before the work starts

A mobile appointment begins before the detailer touches the vehicle. Loading equipment, driving to the customer, finding the location, setting up hoses or power, arranging water and preparing chemicals all consume time or resources. If the price only reflects hands-on detailing time, mobile travel can quietly erase margin.

Track door-to-door economics. Separate travel time from vehicle running cost so both can be recovered where appropriate.

Vehicle condition matters more than package name

Two vehicles booked for the same package can require very different labour. Pet hair, sand, stains, neglected wheels, tree sap, heavy bug residue or excessive interior clutter can increase time dramatically. Vehicle size matters too, but condition can be the larger variable.

Use package names to define the standard scope, then adjust the time estimate for vehicle size and condition. Avoid vague surcharges that are difficult to explain. Price the extra work through realistic extra minutes and product use.

Build labour from door-to-door hours

Calculate paid labour for setup, detailing, pack-down and travel. If two detailers work together, multiply the elapsed working time by crew size. A four-hour detail with two people is eight labour hours before travel if both are working throughout.

Use a loaded labour cost that reflects wages or owner time plus employer on-costs where relevant.

Direct detailing costs add up

Shampoo, APC, wheel cleaner, tyre dressing, glass cleaner, interior protectant, decontamination products, towels, pads and applicators all create cost. Water and electricity may be supplied by the customer on some jobs, while other mobile setups carry their own water tank, generator or battery system.

Equipment wear also belongs in the model. Extractors, pressure washers, vacuums, polishers, hoses and batteries eventually need service or replacement.

Travel should be recovered deliberately

There is no single correct way to show travel to the customer. A business can build it into package pricing, use zones, charge beyond a radius or price travel individually. What matters is that the internal quote includes both paid travel time and vehicle running cost where those apply.

Long-distance appointments can look attractive because the ticket value is high, but the drive can reduce the number of jobs available that day. Capacity cost is part of the commercial decision.

A practical mobile detailing pricing sequence

  1. Choose the service scope and assess vehicle size and condition.
  2. Estimate hands-on time, setup, pack-down and paid travel.
  3. Calculate loaded labour cost.
  4. Add chemicals, water, power, towels, pads and equipment wear.
  5. Add vehicle cost for the trip.
  6. Allocate monthly overhead across realistic paid jobs.
  7. Add transaction fees and contingency where relevant.
  8. Apply the target retained margin and minimum charge.
  9. Add tax only where required.

Minimum charges protect mobile capacity

A low-value job still requires travel, loading and setup. A minimum mobile charge prevents a short service from occupying a profitable time slot without recovering the cost of getting to the customer. The minimum should be based on the business's own travel pattern, labour and overhead rather than a copied local figure.

Common mobile detailing pricing mistakes

  • charging for hands-on time but not travel or setup
  • using one package price regardless of vehicle condition
  • ignoring equipment and towel replacement
  • absorbing long-distance jobs without checking daily capacity
  • using owner labour as free time
  • confusing markup with retained margin
