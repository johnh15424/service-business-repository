FOR PAINTING & DECORATING BUSINESSES

How to price interior painting from preparation through final coat

This guide is for decorators building profitable interior quotes, not homeowners comparing painting prices. The quote should recover preparation, protection, labour, materials, trim, access, clean-up and overhead before the target margin is applied.

Start with scope before measuring walls

Interior painting can include walls only, walls and ceilings, woodwork, doors, radiators, repairs, stain blocking and furniture protection. Define the included surfaces before estimating hours or materials. A room count without scope detail is too vague to price consistently.

Preparation often controls the margin

Filling, sanding, caulking, masking, scraping and stain blocking can consume substantial labour before the first finish coat. Poor preparation estimates are a common reason apparently simple decorating jobs overrun.

Inspect walls and woodwork carefully. Where damage is uncertain, state assumptions and define how additional repair work will be handled.

Break labour into stages

Estimate time for protection and setup, preparation, first coat, subsequent coats, trim and final cleanup. Add movement between rooms, access equipment and drying-stage logistics where they affect paid time.

Use loaded labour cost, not wage alone, and include owner labour realistically.

Materials should reflect coverage and coats

Estimate paint from measured area, expected coverage and the number of coats. Add primer, undercoat, filler, caulk, masking tape, protective film, abrasives, rollers and other sundries. Allow sensible wastage rather than assuming every litre reaches the surface.

Trim and detail work can dominate a room

Doors, frames, skirting, architraves, window boards and radiators add cutting-in and finish work without adding much wall area. Price them separately or estimate their labour explicitly. Do not rely on wall square metres to recover detail work.

Occupied properties reduce productivity

Furniture movement, room-by-room sequencing, customer access and additional protection can slow work compared with an empty property. Build that into the time estimate rather than assuming new-build productivity on an occupied home or office.

A practical interior-painting pricing sequence

  1. Define walls, ceilings, trim and exclusions.
  2. Assess repairs, preparation and access.
  3. Estimate labour by stage.
  4. Calculate paint, primer and sundry requirements.
  5. Add equipment, travel and protection costs.
  6. Allocate business overhead.
  7. Add fees and contingency where appropriate.
  8. Apply the retained margin target.
  9. Add tax only where required.

Use a day rate only as an internal check

Some decorators think naturally in days. That can be useful, but the day rate still needs to fund labour, non-billable time, vehicle, tools, insurance and overhead. After building the job price, compare it with expected painter-days to check the effective return.

Common interior-painting pricing mistakes

  • measuring walls but ignoring preparation
  • forgetting woodwork and detailed cutting-in
  • assuming two coats are always enough without checking substrate and colour change
  • underpricing occupied-property setup and protection
  • pricing from litres of paint rather than labour
  • copying a per-room rate from another decorator
