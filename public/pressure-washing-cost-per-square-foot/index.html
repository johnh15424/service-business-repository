FOR PRESSURE WASHING BUSINESSES

How to calculate pressure washing cost per square foot

This page is for contractors building profitable unit rates, not homeowners looking for a local quote. A square-foot price should be the output of the costing model, not a number copied from a competitor or an online average.

Start with the job cost, then calculate the unit rate

Pressure washing businesses often need a square-foot rate for estimates, tenders and repeat commercial work. The safest way to build that rate is to cost the entire job first. Labour, setup, travel, chemicals, fuel, equipment wear and overhead are all real costs even when the customer only sees a simple price per square foot.

Once the required pre-tax selling price is known, divide it by the measured square footage. That produces a unit rate that reflects the economics of the business rather than a generic market figure.

The basic formula

Pressure washing price per square foot = required pre-tax job price ÷ total square footage.

The required job price should already recover direct costs, allocated overhead, payment fees and the retained margin target. If the business has a minimum charge, use whichever is higher: the calculated price or the minimum.

For businesses that quote in square metres, the method is identical. Keep the area unit consistent from measurement through to the final unit rate.

Labour productivity drives the rate

Square footage alone cannot tell you the cost of a job because production speed changes by surface, condition and method. Smooth open concrete may be cleaned quickly with a surface cleaner. Detailed paving, steps, walls or areas with heavy organic growth can require slower lance work, pre-treatment or post-treatment.

Estimate the actual crew-hours first. If two technicians spend three hours on site, that is six labour hours. Add paid setup, pack-down and travel where appropriate. Multiply total paid hours by the loaded labour cost, not just the wage rate.

Do not hide fixed costs inside an unrealistically low unit rate

Travel, loading, site setup and administration create costs before the first square foot is cleaned. On a large site those fixed costs are spread across many units. On a small site they can dominate the economics. That is why many contractors need a minimum call-out or mobilisation charge.

When comparing unit rates across jobs, separate fixed mobilisation from variable area-based work where useful. A contractor may use a base charge plus a unit rate rather than forcing every cost into one number.

Build separate rate families for materially different work

One universal square-foot rate is rarely robust. Concrete, block paving, facades, decking and specialist treatments can have different labour and consumable profiles. Commercial loading bays with oil contamination may require chemistry and dwell time that an ordinary path does not.

Use separate internal rate bands when the delivery method changes. The point is not to create dozens of arbitrary price lists. It is to prevent a fast, low-cost surface from subsidising a slow, high-cost one.

Cost categories to include before dividing by area

  • Labour: on-site work, setup, pack-down and paid travel.
  • Chemicals: detergents, degreasers, biocides and specialist treatments.
  • Utilities: water, generator fuel and power where borne by the contractor.
  • Equipment wear: pumps, hoses, reels, nozzles, surface cleaners and service intervals.
  • Vehicle cost: mileage or kilometre cost separate from paid travel time.
  • Overhead: insurance, storage, software, marketing, admin and compliance costs.
  • Fees and contingency: predictable payment charges, rework and leakage.
  • Margin: the retained margin needed after the relevant operating costs.

Example methodology without using a fake market rate

Suppose a contractor estimates a commercial wash and, after labour, chemicals, travel, overhead, fees and margin, the required pre-tax price is 1,200 in the business's currency. If the measured area is 6,000 square feet, the internal unit rate is 0.20 per square foot. The useful part of the example is the calculation method, not the number. A different contractor with different labour cost, equipment, travel or margin could legitimately arrive at a very different rate.

This is why Service Pricing Tools does not publish a universal recommended square-foot price. The commercially useful figure is the rate that works for the business actually doing the job.

Common unit-rate mistakes

  • copying a competitor's public price without knowing their cost base
  • using square footage while forgetting setup and mobilisation
  • assuming one productivity rate across every surface
  • charging owner labour at zero
  • failing to recover chemical usage on treatment-heavy jobs
  • mixing up markup and retained margin
  • forgetting minimum charges on small jobs
