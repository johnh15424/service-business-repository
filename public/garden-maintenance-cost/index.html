FOR GARDEN MAINTENANCE BUSINESSES

How to price garden maintenance when every visit has a different task mix

This guide is for garden maintenance businesses setting service prices, not homeowners comparing gardeners. Mixed visits should be priced from total crew-hours, machinery, waste, travel and overhead rather than one rough hourly figure.

Mixed maintenance work needs a job-level model

A maintenance visit may combine mowing, edging, hedge trimming, shrub work, weeding, leaf clearance and waste removal. Those tasks have different productivity and equipment requirements. A single hourly price can work, but only if the business knows the full hourly cost behind it.

For fixed-price visits, estimate the time by task and combine the crew-hours into one quote. This makes it easier to spot when a seasonal task such as leaf clearance is turning a profitable maintenance visit into an overrun.

Seasonality changes the workload

Garden maintenance is not equally demanding every month. Spring growth, autumn leaves, wet conditions and hedge seasons can increase labour. A recurring contract should therefore define the service scope and frequency clearly rather than promising unlimited garden work for one static price.

Businesses can use seasonal schedules, task rotations or agreed extra-work rates to keep recurring work commercially sustainable.

Machinery cost varies by task

Mowing uses different equipment from hedge cutting, leaf clearance or pruning. Fuel, batteries, blades, trimmer line, chains, servicing and replacement should be allocated sensibly across the work that creates the wear. If a task needs specialist machinery, include that directly rather than expecting the base maintenance price to absorb it.

Waste handling deserves its own line in the model

Green waste can create loading time, vehicle capacity constraints, disposal charges and an additional trip. Some customers have suitable composting or green bins, while others expect everything removed. Clarify the disposal expectation before quoting.

Travel and route density affect recurring margins

Two one-hour maintenance visits can have very different economics if one sits beside the previous customer and the other requires 35 minutes of driving. Route planning is therefore part of pricing. Track paid travel time and vehicle cost, then review whether remote customers need a higher minimum or travel adjustment.

A practical garden maintenance pricing sequence

  1. Define the recurring or one-off task scope.
  2. Estimate crew-hours by task.
  3. Add loaded labour cost.
  4. Add fuel, consumables and equipment wear.
  5. Add green-waste handling and disposal where relevant.
  6. Add travel and vehicle cost.
  7. Allocate monthly overhead across realistic paid work.
  8. Apply fees, contingency, minimum charge and retained margin.
  9. Add tax only where required.

Common garden-maintenance pricing mistakes

  • promising an undefined list of tasks for one fixed price
  • using summer productivity assumptions during heavy seasonal growth
  • absorbing waste removal
  • forgetting tool and machinery wear
  • not recovering travel between scattered customers
  • failing to review recurring jobs after the actual workload becomes clear
