FOR JUNK REMOVAL BUSINESSES

Junk Removal Cost per Load: Build Load-Based Prices from Real Costs

Load fractions are easy for customers to understand, but the contractor should calculate the required job price first. That keeps a quarter-load or half-load price tied to crew time, vehicle capacity, disposal cost and margin.

Do not start with the load fraction

A half-load job is not automatically half the cost of a full load. Small jobs may carry nearly the same travel and setup cost, while heavy material can create high disposal cost without using much visible volume. Build the cost model first, then translate the result into a load-based customer price.

The variables behind load pricing

Crew time

Long carries, dismantling, stairs and sorting can make a smaller load more labour-intensive than a larger easy-access load.

Disposal basis

If facilities charge by weight or material type, volume alone cannot predict disposal cost reliably.

Vehicle capacity

Know the usable volume and weight limits of the vehicle or trailer. Revenue per load should be measured against the capacity actually available.

Travel and unloading

Each run includes movement to the customer and from the customer to the disposal or transfer point. That time and mileage belong in the job.

How to set a per-load price

  1. Estimate crew person-hours.
  2. Add travel and vehicle cost.
  3. Add expected disposal cost.
  4. Add equipment, PPE and other direct costs.
  5. Add overhead allocation and payment fees.
  6. Apply retained margin.
  7. Compare the result with the fraction of usable load capacity consumed.
  8. Create load bands only after enough completed jobs support the assumptions.

Use a minimum for small pickups

A single-item pickup can consume travel, scheduling and disposal time out of proportion to its volume. A minimum charge protects those fixed costs without forcing every small job into an unrealistic load fraction.

Common load-pricing mistakes

  • Using visible volume without considering weight.
  • Assuming disposal cost scales evenly with truck space.
  • Ignoring crew time on difficult-access jobs.
  • Offering load discounts before actual capacity data exists.
  • Failing to separate hazardous or restricted materials from normal pricing.
