FOR PAINTERS & DECORATORS

How to calculate painting cost per square metre without underpricing preparation

This guide is for painting businesses building profitable rates, not homeowners comparing decoration quotes. A square-metre figure is useful only after preparation, labour productivity, coats, materials, access and overhead have been costed properly.

Square metres describe area, not difficulty

Two rooms can have the same wall area but require very different labour. One may need light filling and two straightforward coats. Another may have damaged plaster, stains, peeling paint, detailed cutting-in, high ceilings or awkward access. If both use the same area rate, one is likely to subsidise the other.

Use measured area as a quoting unit after the production method is understood.

Preparation should be priced separately in the model

Filling, sanding, caulking, stain blocking, scraping, masking and surface repair often consume more labour than rolling the finish coats. Estimate preparation hours explicitly instead of hiding them inside an optimistic square-metre productivity rate.

Where surface defects are uncertain, define an allowance and exclusions so extensive repair does not become unlimited work inside a fixed painting price.

Coat count affects both labour and material use

Primer, mist coat, undercoat and finish coats should be reflected in the plan. Colour changes, porous surfaces and poor coverage can add coats. Estimate litres from actual coverage assumptions and wastage, then add sundries such as tape, fillers, abrasives, rollers, sleeves and protection materials.

Labour productivity should be based on the business's own work

Track completed square metres per painter-hour for comparable tasks. Separate straightforward rolling from detailed rooms, stairwells, trim-heavy work and high-access jobs. Over time, these records form a useful internal rate library that is more reliable than a generic market figure.

Trim and detail work should not disappear into wall area

Doors, frames, skirting, architraves, windows, radiators and detailed cutting-in can add significant time with little additional wall area. Either price those items separately or include their labour explicitly before calculating the blended square-metre rate.

A practical square-metre pricing method

  1. Measure the paintable wall or ceiling area.
  2. Define preparation and coat requirements.
  3. Estimate preparation, application and detail labour.
  4. Add paint, sundries and equipment or access cost.
  5. Add travel and site-specific costs.
  6. Allocate business overhead.
  7. Apply fees, contingency and retained margin.
  8. Divide the required pre-tax price by the measured area.
  9. Add tax only where required.

Use separate unit rates for materially different work

It can be useful to maintain different internal benchmarks for walls, ceilings, new plaster, previously painted surfaces or commercial open-plan work. The aim is not to create a complicated price book, but to prevent a fast surface from masking the cost of a slow one.

Common square-metre pricing mistakes

  • ignoring preparation time
  • using one productivity rate for walls, ceilings and trim
  • forgetting additional coats and primer
  • underestimating access and room protection
  • pricing material at purchase cost but forgetting sundries and wastage
  • copying a market rate without checking labour productivity
