DOG GROOMING PRICE LIST GUIDE

A price list should make
the difficult jobs visible.

A useful service menu is simple for customers but detailed enough behind the scenes to protect your time, cost and margin.

Build your own prices ↗

Keep the customer menu simpler than the internal cost model

Customers do not need to see every cost category. Internally, however, you should know how long each service takes, what products it uses, how much equipment wear it creates, how much overhead it must carry and what minimum price protects the appointment.

The public price list can stay clean while the internal model remains detailed.

Use service type as the first layer

Separate genuinely different services such as bath and brush, full groom, deshedding, puppy introduction, nail trim and hand stripping. They have different time and skill profiles, so forcing them into one base price makes the menu harder to control.

Use dog size carefully

Size can be a useful starting band, but it is not a complete pricing method. Coat density, coat length, condition, temperament and the requested finish can matter as much as body weight.

Use size bands to make the menu understandable, then keep the right to adjust for actual time and condition.

Separate coat-condition surcharges from normal maintenance

A maintained coat should not silently subsidise a heavily tangled or matted appointment. Extra coat work consumes time, products and often more blade wear. Make that difference visible in either a surcharge, an hourly extra-work policy or a quote-after-assessment policy.

Safety and welfare come first; pricing should never incentivise unnecessarily prolonged dematting.

Set minimums for short services

Nail trims, puppy introductions and other short services still use booking, setup, cleaning and payment time. A minimum charge helps make sure those appointments are worth accepting.

For mobile grooming, add geography to the menu logic

Mobile groomers may need service-area rules, travel zones or minimum stop values because the same groom can have very different economics depending on route time. See the mobile pricing guide.

Review the menu with real appointment data

Every month or quarter, compare planned service duration with actual completed appointment time. If a service repeatedly overruns, update the internal assumption before changing the public price.

This is especially useful for breeds or coat types that appear frequently in your diary.

Keep tax separate from the advertised business price logic

How prices must be displayed can vary by jurisdiction and customer type. Internally, keep the pre-tax business economics separate from tax collected for the relevant authority. Verify the local display and registration rules that apply to you.

Build the menu from cost, then sense-check the market

Competitor prices are useful context, but they cannot tell you whether your own labour, rent, capacity or service quality are covered. First calculate the price your business requires. Then decide how the offer should be positioned.
